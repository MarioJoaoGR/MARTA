\section{Results and Discussion}

In this section we compare MARTA against the single-prompt baseline (Test4Py) and
the search-based generator (Pynguin) across the 27 projects (486 modules) of our
corpus. As described in Section~\ref{sec:implementation}, MARTA and the
single-prompt baseline share the identical Phase~1 semantic context, the same
underlying model, and a repair budget of $N=3$; the results below report the full
pipeline with its outer coverage-driven loop enabled (three generation rounds).

\subsection{Test Suite Yield and Oracle Strength}

A recurring claim in LLM-based test generation is that richer context yields more
executable tests. Our data show that, at this model scale, the decisive difference
between architectures lies not in \emph{how many} tests survive validation, but in
\emph{what those tests actually verify}.

Both LLM-based pipelines converge on suites of comparable size: MARTA retains
11{,}255 test functions and the single-prompt baseline 11{,}235, while Pynguin
produces 4{,}100. The distinguishing factor is oracle strength. As shown in
Table~\ref{tab:oracles}, only 0.8\% of MARTA's tests contain no assertion at all,
against 7.1\% for the single-prompt baseline and 38.3\% for Pynguin. The baseline's
rate is a direct, measurable consequence of its \emph{heuristic reduction} step,
which minimizes a test until it executes cleanly and thereby removes its assertions;
MARTA, by contrast, discards unrepairable tests outright rather than hollowing them
out (Section~3.2.4). Pynguin's much higher rate reflects a different mechanism: as a
search-based generator optimizing structural coverage, a substantial portion of its
test cases exist purely to execute code paths, with regression assertions attached
only where its assertion generator can produce them.

The same asymmetry appears in assertion quality. We classify an assertion as
\emph{structurally weak} when it cannot discriminate program behaviour: assertions
over constants, \texttt{isinstance}/\texttt{hasattr} checks, comparisons against
\texttt{type(x)} (including the \texttt{f}-string type signatures characteristic of
Pynguin), and assertions over mock internals such as \texttt{call\_count}. Under this
deliberately conservative criterion, which does \emph{not} penalize comparisons
against literal values, 13.1\% of MARTA's assertions are weak, against 17.0\% for the
baseline and 30.4\% for Pynguin.

\texttt{All.concat} from \texttt{pyMonet}, for which all three tools produced tests.
Both LLM-driven pipelines name their tests after the scenario they encode and assert

\texttt{test\_case\_0..n} whose assertions are dominated by type signatures and by
attributes fixed at construction time, and several of its tests carry
\texttt{@pytest.mark.xfail}, recording only that an exception occurs rather than what
correct behaviour would be. Such tests execute the code under test and therefore
contribute to coverage, but they encode no expectation that a regression could
violate.

\subsection{Structural Coverage}

The benchmark defines 486 \emph{modules} as its units of evaluation
\cite{lemieux2023codamosa}, and we adopt that unit as our primary aggregation. This
choice is consequential, because the projects differ by more than two orders of
magnitude in size: \texttt{codetiming} contributes a single module, whereas
\texttt{ansible} alone contributes 237, that is, 49\% of the benchmark. Averaging
percentages over projects would therefore weight a one-module utility as heavily as a
237-module framework. We consequently treat each module as one observation and report
its median and mean as our primary results, together with a pooled measure as a
weighting-free confirmation; the per-project view is retained as a secondary
perspective and analysed separately below. The pooled measure is the strictest of the
three: computed as the ratio of all covered lines and branches to all coverable ones,
it uses an identical denominator (68{,}248 coverable elements) for the three tools and
involves no weighting choice at all. Reporting the per-module median additionally
allows the search-based baseline to be calibrated against published figures for the
same benchmark (Section~5.5).

At the module level MARTA ranks first on all three measures, and it improves over
the single-prompt baseline under every aggregation, winning 19 of the 27 pairwise
per-project comparisons. Averaged over projects, however, Pynguin ranks first, and
the discrepancy between the two views is itself the finding. Pynguin's advantage is
concentrated in small, structurally simple modules, where an evolutionary search can
effectively exhaust the input space: it reaches 100\% on \texttt{sty} (two modules)
and 96.2\% on \texttt{codetiming} (one module). Because such projects are numerous
but contribute few modules each, they dominate a per-project average while
representing a small fraction of the benchmark. On the largest and most complex
subjects the ordering reverses: on \texttt{ansible} MARTA attains 40.0\% against
Pynguin's 27.7\%, on \texttt{flutils} 66.6\% against 33.6\%, and on \texttt{black}
Pynguin covers none of the target modules. This is precisely the coverage plateau
that motivated augmenting search with LLMs \cite{lemieux2023codamosa}: evolutionary
search saturates where the state space is large and valid object construction is
non-trivial, which is the regime in which semantic context pays off. We therefore
read the two views as complementary rather than contradictory: search-based
generation remains the stronger choice for small, self-contained modules, while the
multi-agent pipeline scales better to the complex subjects that dominate the
benchmark by module count.

\subsection{Fault-Revealing Capability}

High coverage is a well-known vanity metric: executing a line does not imply
detecting a fault in it. We therefore assess the generated suites with mutation
analysis. Because the projects differ in size, mutants are attributed to the module
they belong to, and modules for which a tool produced no usable test contribute their
mutants as survived rather than being excluded, so that a tool is not rewarded for
generating fewer tests.

Over the [[MUTMUT-ANSIBLE: 23]] projects for which all three tools yield a mutation
score, MARTA achieves a mean score of 26.5\% against 25.0\% for the single-prompt
baseline and 21.9\% for Pynguin, and obtains the highest score on 13 projects
against 6 and 7 respectively. The comparison with Pynguin is the more informative
one: Pynguin attains higher per-project coverage yet detects fewer injected faults,
which is consistent with the oracle measurements in Table~\ref{tab:oracles}. A suite
in which 38.3\% of tests assert nothing accumulates structural coverage without
acquiring the ability to detect a regression.

\subsection{Computational Cost}

Decoupling generation into two agents plus a repair loop could reasonably be expected
to multiply inference cost. To assess this we separate the two phases, since they are
not architecturally comparable: Phase~1 is a shared context-building pipeline that
MARTA inherits from the single-prompt baseline and that both systems execute
identically, whereas Phase~2 is where the architectures actually differ. Conflating
them is misleading, particularly because Phase~1 results are cached and keyed by a
content hash of the source, so a run over unchanged code restores them at negligible
cost.

Measured separately over the 27 projects, the shared analysis phase accounts for
21.0 GPU-hours when executed from scratch. The generation phase, which is the
architectural variable, costs 40.6 GPU-hours for MARTA against 81.7 for the
single-prompt baseline: the decoupled pipeline generates its suites in half the time,
despite issuing an additional planning call per function. Two design decisions account
for this. First, the Assertion Agent emits one complete file per function in a single
inference call rather than one call per scenario, so the planning call is amortized
across all scenarios of that function. Second, the outer loop regenerates only
functions with remaining uncovered lines, so later rounds are progressively cheaper.
In total tokens, MARTA consumed 119.2M against the baseline's 210.4M; this comparison
is conservative rather than exact, since our logs do not separate tokens by phase and
the baseline's figure therefore includes its Phase~1 summarization while MARTA's run
restored Phase~1 from cache.

Pynguin is excluded from this comparison, as it performs no inference at all; its cost
is CPU search time, fixed at 300\,s per module by configuration.

\subsection{Calibration Against Published Results}

Our conclusions rest on two baselines that we re-execute ourselves under identical
conditions. A natural question is whether those re-executions are faithful to the
systems as published, since a misconfigured baseline would inflate our results
without any architectural merit. The benchmark's shared lineage allows us to check
this directly for the search-based baseline. CodaMosa \cite{lemieux2023codamosa} is
Pynguin augmented with an LLM, and reports a per-module median line+branch coverage
of 47.0\% on this benchmark; our re-execution of Pynguin alone yields 43.1\%. The
residual gap of roughly four percentage points is consistent with the contribution
attributed to the LLM component in the original work, which indicates that our
search-based baseline is configured and instrumented in line with the published
setup rather than being inadvertently handicapped.

The same benchmark also underpins the strongest published result in this line of
work. CoverUp \cite{altmayer2025coverup}, evaluated on a suite of 4{,}116 functions
drawn from these same subjects, reports a per-module median line+branch coverage of
80.0\% and an overall coverage of 60.0\%, against our 44.3\% and 40.6\% respectively.
Because the subjects and the reported quantities coincide, these figures are
comparable as \emph{end-to-end system} results, and we report them as such.

They are not, however, comparable as a measurement of architecture. CoverUp is driven
by GPT-4o, whereas every system we execute is driven by a locally served
open-weights model that activates 2.4B parameters per token, and we were unable to
execute CoverUp in our environment, so its configuration is outside our control. The
gap therefore conflates the effect of the generation architecture with the effect of
model capacity, and we make no architectural claim from it. That the two effects are
separable at all is suggested by Pynguin, which performs no inference and is thus
invariant to model choice: it lands within two percentage points of our LLM-driven
systems at this model scale, while the published LLM-based results span more than
thirty. Section~\ref{sec:model-capacity} probes this directly by holding the
architecture fixed and varying only the model.
